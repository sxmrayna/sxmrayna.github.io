Master's thesis · 2020 · Human–computer interaction

Deep Talk with AI

The effects of Need for Cognition on deep vs. shallow conversations with artificial agents

Can a conversation with a chatbot feel meaningful, and does a person's enjoyment of thinking shape that experience?

My master's thesis compared deep and shallow conversations with a scripted chatbot. Participants wrote longer responses and evaluated the deep condition more positively overall. The evaluation difference was most apparent among participants with lower Need for Cognition, challenging the expectation that deeper talk would appeal more to those who enjoy effortful thinking.

From 36 questions to talking with AI

Between people

36 Questions to Fall in Love {% include new-tab-arrow.html %}

The “36 Questions to Fall in Love” were one of the starting points for my thesis. In Aron and colleagues’ closeness research, two people take turns answering questions that gradually become more personal. What interested me was the movement from everyday facts toward memories, values, and feelings—and the way each person’s disclosure invites a response from the other.

Between a person and a voice

Her (2013) {% include new-tab-arrow.html %}

In Spike Jonze’s film Her, Theodore develops a relationship with Samantha, an artificial voice. It made the question vivid: can someone feel listened to and connected when the other side of the conversation is a system?

For my study, this became a question about the experience of talking to a chatbot: how do people engage with a more personal script, and when does its apparent understanding feel convincing?

Watch the official trailer {% include new-tab-arrow.html %}

Together, these examples led me from the idea of connection to two conversation-design choices I could examine: gradual escalation and reciprocal self-disclosure. I translated them into two 20-question scripts and compared how people engaged with and evaluated the conversations.

Research question

The study asked how conversational depth relates to engagement and evaluation, and whether this relationship differs with Need for Cognition (NFC). NFC describes a person's tendency to engage in and enjoy effortful thinking. It concerns motivation to think, rather than a measure of intelligence.

I expected deeper topics to appeal especially to participants with higher NFC. The analysis also examined how feeling understood and perceived conversational depth were associated with participants' evaluations.

Study design

The final analytic sample included 89 participants. The study combined an NFC survey with a comparison of two chatbot conditions. Both conditions used 20 questions, the same first 10 questions, and prewritten chatbot replies. The final 10 questions created the contrast in conversational depth.

The analysis considered three outcomes: the number of questions answered, the length of responses, and participants' evaluation of the conversation. Ratings of feeling understood and perceived conversational depth helped interpret the experience.

The chatbot followed a predefined script. Its apparent self-disclosure and empathic replies were part of the conversation design.

Findings

Longer responses in the deep condition

Participants gave longer responses in the deep condition, and their evaluations were generally more positive. The number of questions answered varied little, with most observations at the maximum shown in the chart. Response length therefore captured a difference that completion alone did not reveal.

The response-length comparison also showed longer responses in the deep condition within both NFC groups. Writing more, however, does not by itself establish that a person felt satisfied or connected.

The evaluation difference varied with NFC

The lower-NFC group evaluated the deep condition more positively than the shallow condition. In the higher-NFC group, average evaluations were similar across conditions. This pattern ran counter to the expectation that deeper conversation would be especially appealing to higher-NFC participants.

Feeling understood accompanied better evaluations

Across both conditions, feeling more understood was associated with a more positive evaluation. Within the deep condition, the lower-NFC group reported feeling more understood on average than the higher-NFC group. These patterns offer a possible interpretation of the evaluation difference, rather than proof of a causal mechanism.

Perceived conversational depth showed a related pattern. Participants who perceived the conversation as deeper tended to evaluate it more positively. The lower-NFC group also rated the deep condition as deeper on average than the higher-NFC group. A script's intended depth and a participant's experience of that depth are therefore distinct parts of the design.

Interpretation and limitations

The findings suggest that reflective topics can encourage more extensive responses, while a positive experience also involves whether the participant feels understood. Some participants recognized the chatbot's predefined replies and questioned its apparent understanding or empathy. This feedback highlights a limitation of using a fixed script for personal conversation.

The group comparisons describe this sample and this chatbot. They do not establish a general preference for chatbots among people with lower NFC, or an inability of chatbots to engage people with higher NFC. The associations between feeling understood, perceived depth, and evaluation also do not establish which experience caused another.

The study examined a bounded interaction with a scripted agent. It does not show whether the same patterns persist in a long-term relationship or with a contemporary generative AI system. The overlapping response distributions also show variation within each NFC group, so these group patterns should not be used to predict an individual's experience.

Implications for conversational design

The results suggest several directions for designing and evaluating conversational agents:

  • Give users control over depth. Gradually introducing reflective topics can support disclosure, while options to skip or change the subject let users choose how personal the conversation becomes.
  • Attend to the response as well as the question. A personal prompt creates expectations of understanding. Replies need to acknowledge what a user actually shared, and scripted expressions of empathy can fall short of that expectation.
  • Evaluate behavior and experience separately. Completion and response length reveal different aspects of engagement. Neither replaces asking whether a person felt understood or valued the conversation.

My thesis connected a concrete conversation-design choice with individual differences and subjective experience. Its contribution is an empirical comparison of conversational depth that also shows why richer disclosure and a more satisfying interaction require separate attention.
