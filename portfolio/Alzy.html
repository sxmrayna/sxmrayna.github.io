Our team explored how technology could support more personal care for people living with Alzheimer's disease, especially when expressing preferences becomes difficult.

01 / The challenge

Keeping the person
in the picture.

A caregiver may look after several people, each with a different history, routine and way of expressing themselves. Knowing these details can shape the everyday choices made together.

We focused on a tool for professional caregivers: a place to consult personal background, understand familiar activities, and coordinate appointments. The concept also explored how an indication of current mood might help caregivers notice when an activity needs attention.

Alzy was an academic design prototype. The project explored a care workflow and interface; it did not establish clinical effectiveness or validate emotion detection.

02 / Listening & learning

Start with everyday life.

Desk research and stakeholder mapping helped us frame the relationships between affected people, relatives and caregivers. Interviews brought us closer to everyday experiences of remembering, communication and care.

We organized the material through an affinity diagram, first with physical notes and then digitally. Rather than beginning with an app feature, we used these observations to decide whom the product should support and where it could fit.

01

Information has a human source.

Relatives and familiar caregivers know a person's background and interests. New caregivers need a way to access that context.

02

The interface should reduce demands.

Our research led us toward supporting caregivers, rather than requiring people with advanced symptoms to learn a new tool.

03

Preferences still matter.

The design opportunity became helping caregivers understand the individual when feelings and wishes are difficult to communicate.

03 / Turning insights into a concept

Context for small,
meaningful choices.

Storyboards explored how a caregiver might get to know someone, choose an activity, and respond to their experience. These scenarios developed into connected views for personal profiles, activities and calendars.

Personal history

Keep background and familiar interests close to the daily care workflow.

Activities & routines

Connect preferred activities with appointments and the day's schedule.

Mood at a glance

Explore visual cues that prompt attention and a conversation with the person.

The proposed workflow linked biography and activity preferences with planning, then brought current mood into the activity view. Wearable sensing was an exploratory part of the concept, rather than a proven measure of a person's feelings.

05 / Feedback & refinement

Support choice.
Keep agency visible.

Feedback from a psychomotor therapist shifted our attention toward how the interface represents the person. A useful suggestion should support a conversation about what someone wants to do.

  • Ask, then confirm. Prompt the caregiver to check the person's preference before confirming an activity.
  • Make the choice manageable. Present two activity suggestions rather than an overwhelming set of options.
  • Put personal history first. Bring life history ahead of medical history in the profile, so the person is introduced through more than a condition.

The most important refinement was a change in emphasis: helping a caregiver know and ask the person, alongside organizing information about them.
